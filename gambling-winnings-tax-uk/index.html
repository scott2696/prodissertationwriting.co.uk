Tax on Gambling Winnings UK: Why You Pay Nothing

UK gambling winnings are not taxable. Not at £100, not at £100,000, not from a UK-licensed site and not from an offshore one. There is no threshold, no declaration and nothing to enter on a self-assessment return. That is the answer to the question almost everyone arrives here asking — and the rest of this page is about the two narrow circumstances in which money connected to gambling can become taxable.

The short answer: You pay no tax on gambling winnings in the UK. Betting winnings are not income for income tax purposes, and they are expressly excluded from capital gains tax. The licensing jurisdiction of the operator makes no difference: a win from a Curaçao-licensed casino is exactly as untaxed as a win from a UK-licensed one, because the tax treatment attaches to the nature of the receipt rather than to the payer. Two things can still create a liability, and neither is the win itself: gambling carried on as a trade (rare, and harder to establish than people assume), and cryptocurrency held after a withdrawal, where a later conversion is a disposal for capital gains purposes.

Why gambling is untaxed in the UK

The position rests on two long-standing principles rather than on a single exemption.

First, betting winnings are not income. Income tax applies to income from employment, trade, property, savings and investments. A gambling win falls into none of those categories: it is the result of a wager, not a return on labour or capital. The point traces back to nineteenth-century case law — Graham v Green being the case usually cited — establishing that habitually betting, even successfully, is not by itself carrying on a trade.

Second, betting is expressly excluded from capital gains tax. Statute provides that winnings from betting, including pool betting and lotteries and games with prizes, are not chargeable gains.

The reason the Treasury is content with this is straightforward: it taxes the other side of the transaction. Operators pay a general betting duty, remote gaming duty and machine games duty, and since the 2014 point-of-consumption reforms those apply to operators taking bets from British customers wherever the operator sits. The revenue is collected from the bookmaker, in bulk, at source. Taxing players individually would also imply allowing loss relief, which would be both administratively enormous and, for the exchequer, an extremely bad deal.

Does an offshore operator change anything?

No. This is the question I am asked most often about tax, and the answer is clean: the treatment attaches to the nature of the receipt, not to the identity or location of the payer. A win is a betting win whether it comes from a UK-licensed operator, a Curaçao-licensed one or a private wager between friends. None of it is taxable income.

What an offshore operator does change is everything else — your complaints route, your self-exclusion coverage, how your money is held. Those are set out on the non-GamStop page. Tax is simply not among the differences.

Exception one: gambling as a trade

The phrase “professional gambler” leads people to assume there is a point at which winning enough makes you taxable. There is not, and the threshold people imagine does not exist.

HMRC's long-standing position is that gambling is not a trade even where it is conducted systematically and profitably, because the element of chance prevents the organised activity from amounting to a trade in the ordinary sense. A poker player who plays full-time and profitably is generally still not trading, and their winnings are generally still untaxed.

Where it does change

The picture shifts when income arises from something other than the wagering itself — sponsorship, appearance fees, streaming or media income, coaching, selling tips or running a syndicate for a fee, or being employed to gamble for someone else. That money is trading or employment income and is taxable in the ordinary way. The distinction is not “how much you win”, it is “are you being paid for something other than the bet”. If any of that applies to you, this page is not enough and you should speak to an accountant.

Exception two: cryptocurrency

This one catches real people with real money and is almost never mentioned on pages about crypto casinos.

HMRC treats cryptoassets as property rather than as currency. So when you withdraw winnings in Bitcoin, two separate things have happened: you have received an untaxed gambling win, and you have acquired an asset at its sterling value on that day. If you later sell that asset for more than it was worth when you received it, the increase is a capital gain.

How a tax-free win can produce a taxable gain
EventValueTax treatment
Win and withdraw 0.1 BTC when BTC = £50,000£5,000Not taxable. Gambling win. Acquisition cost of the asset is £5,000.
Hold for three months; BTC rises to £65,000£6,500Nothing yet. An unrealised gain is not a disposal.

Alternative: sell immediately on withdrawal£5,000Negligible gain. Nothing meaningful to report.

Note also that swapping one cryptocurrency for another is itself a disposal, not merely a transfer — converting BTC to USDT is a taxable event even though no pounds are involved. And a fall in value produces a loss, which can be useful but still needs to be recorded.

Two practical conclusions. Record the sterling value of every crypto withdrawal on the day it arrives; that figure is your acquisition cost and reconstructing it later is miserable. And if you would rather not think about any of this, use a stablecoin and convert promptly — a dollar-pegged coin sold within days has essentially nothing to report.

What is taxable: interest on the money afterwards

Once winnings are in your bank account they are simply money, and money earns interest. That interest is taxable savings income in the ordinary way, subject to your personal savings allowance. A large win sitting in a savings account can therefore generate a tax liability even though the win itself never did.

Similarly, if you invest winnings, the returns on that investment are taxable like any other investment returns. The tax-free treatment covers the win, not everything the money subsequently does.

What records to keep, and why

You are not required to report gambling winnings, so strictly you need no records for tax purposes. Two practical reasons to keep them anyway:

  • Explaining a large deposit to your bank. Banks ask about unusual credits under anti-money-laundering obligations, and “I won it” is far more persuasive with a withdrawal confirmation and an account statement attached. Keep the operator's confirmation for any significant win.
  • Crypto acquisition costs. As above. Date, coin, quantity, sterling value on the day. Ten seconds at the time, hours of work a year later.

A note on mortgage applications and similar: gambling winnings are legitimate funds, but lenders assess regular gambling activity on bank statements when considering affordability. That is a lending decision rather than a tax one, and it is a real consideration for anyone with visible frequent gambling transactions.

Gambling tax: your questions

Do I pay tax on gambling winnings in the UK?

No. Gambling winnings are not taxable income for UK residents, and betting is expressly excluded from capital gains tax. There is no threshold, no declaration requirement and nothing to enter on a self-assessment return, at any amount. The government collects its revenue from operators through gambling duties instead, which is why the player side is left alone.

Do I pay tax on winnings from an offshore casino?

No. The tax treatment attaches to the nature of the receipt rather than to who paid it, so a win from a Curaçao or Anjouan-licensed casino is exactly as untaxed as a win from a UK-licensed one. The operator's jurisdiction changes your consumer protections considerably, but it changes nothing at all about your tax position.

Is there a limit above which winnings become taxable?

No. There is no threshold at any amount. A £10 win and a £10 million win receive identical treatment, because the exclusion is categorical rather than an allowance. This is unlike several other countries — notably the United States, where gambling winnings are taxable income and withholding applies above certain amounts — which is where the confusion usually comes from.

Do professional gamblers pay tax?

Generally not on their winnings. HMRC's long-standing position is that gambling is not a trade even when conducted systematically and profitably, because the element of chance prevents it amounting to a trade. What is taxable is income from something other than the wagering itself: sponsorship, appearance fees, streaming or media income, coaching, or selling tips. If any of that applies to you, take proper advice — the distinction is fact-specific.

Do I pay tax on crypto casino winnings?

Not on the winnings, but possibly on what happens next. HMRC treats cryptoassets as property, so withdrawing in Bitcoin means you have acquired an asset at its sterling value that day. Selling it later for more creates a capital gain, and swapping one coin for another counts as a disposal too. Record the sterling value of each withdrawal on arrival, or use a stablecoin and convert promptly to leave nothing meaningful to report.

Do I need to tell HMRC about a big win?

No. There is no reporting obligation for gambling winnings at any amount, and nothing to include on a self-assessment return. Do keep the operator's withdrawal confirmation, though — not for HMRC but for your bank, which may query a large unusual credit under its anti-money-laundering obligations and will be considerably easier to satisfy with documentation.

Can I claim gambling losses against tax?

No. The exclusion runs both ways: because winnings are not taxable income, losses are not deductible and cannot be offset against anything. This is the logical counterpart of the tax-free treatment and is precisely why the system works the way it does — allowing loss relief on gambling would cost the exchequer far more than taxing winnings would raise.
