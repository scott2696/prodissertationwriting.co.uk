UK Gambling Laws: What the Act Actually Says About You

Written by Priya Raman · Fact-checked by Daniel Mercer · Updated 13 September 2026

Almost every page written about UK gambling law explains the operator's obligations, which is of no practical use to a person deciding where to play. This one is written the other way round. The Gambling Act 2005 places its duties on the people providing gambling, not on the people doing it — and once that is clear, most of the questions people actually ask answer themselves.

The short answer: It is legal for an adult in Great Britain to gamble at an online casino licensed anywhere in the world. The Gambling Act 2005 creates offences for providing facilities for gambling without a licence, and for advertising unlicensed gambling to British consumers — both committed by operators and advertisers, not by customers. There is no player-side offence and never has been. What the law does determine is what happens when something goes wrong: with a UK-licensed operator you have the Gambling Commission, an approved dispute-resolution body and rules on how your money is held. With an offshore operator you have none of those, and that is the whole substance of the choice.

The framework, briefly

Gambling in Great Britain is governed by the Gambling Act 2005, which came into force in 2007 and created the Gambling Commission as the regulator. The Act was substantially extended by the Gambling (Licensing and Advertising) Act 2014, which moved the UK to a point-of-consumption regime: from that point, any operator taking bets from customers in Great Britain needed a UK licence regardless of where it was based.

The Act has three stated licensing objectives, and every rule the Commission makes traces back to one of them:

  • Preventing gambling from being a source of crime or disorder, or being associated with crime
  • Ensuring gambling is conducted in a fair and open way
  • Protecting children and other vulnerable persons from being harmed or exploited by gambling

What the law means for a player

The provisions people ask about

Section 33 makes it an offence to provide facilities for gambling without an operating licence. The offence is committed by the provider. Section 330 makes it an offence to advertise unlicensed gambling to consumers in Great Britain; that offence is committed by the advertiser. There is no provision anywhere in the Act creating an offence for a person who gambles, and no court has ever found otherwise. Age is the one hard limit: gambling below 18 is prohibited, and the operator commits the offence of permitting it.

So the practical answers for a player are short:

  • Can I play at an offshore casino? Yes. No offence.
  • Can I be prosecuted for it? No.
  • Do I pay tax on winnings? No. Duty falls on operators.
  • Can I complain to the Gambling Commission? Only about operators it licenses.
  • Is my money protected? Only to the extent the operator's own regulator requires.

Where offshore operators sit

An operator licensed in Curaçao or Anjouan that accepts customers in Great Britain without a UK licence is, on the face of the Act, providing unlicensed facilities to British consumers. The Commission's enforcement options against a company with no UK presence and no UK assets are limited in practice — it can and does issue warnings, work with payment providers and seek to have sites blocked, but it cannot readily prosecute an entity outside the jurisdiction.

None of that exposes the customer to liability. What it does mean is that the operator's relationship with British law is adversarial rather than cooperative, which is worth understanding when you consider how a dispute with one might go. Your recourse runs to whichever authority issued its licence. Their standing varies considerably, and the comparison is on the non-GamStop page.

Each of these measures makes UK-licensed sites safer and slightly less convenient, and each one therefore increases the commercial attractiveness of the offshore market — which is a large part of why pages like this one exist at all. The protections are real; so is the incentive they create to leave.

Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland

The Gambling Act 2005 applies across Great Britain — England, Scotland and Wales — and gambling is a reserved matter, so Scottish and Welsh players are in exactly the same position as English ones. Local authorities differ on land-based licensing, but online gambling is regulated identically throughout.

Northern Ireland is separate. Gambling there is governed by the Betting, Gaming, Lotteries and Amusements (Northern Ireland) Order 1985, a considerably older framework that was amended in 2022 but still does not license online gambling in the way the Act does. In practice Northern Irish consumers use the same UK-licensed sites, and GamStop covers them, but the statutory framework behind it is different.

UK gambling law: your questions

Is it illegal to play at an offshore casino from the UK?

No. The Gambling Act 2005 creates offences for providing gambling facilities without a licence and for advertising unlicensed gambling to British consumers — both committed by operators and advertisers rather than by customers. There is no player-side offence in the Act. What you lose by playing offshore is protection, not legality: no Gambling Commission complaints route, no approved dispute-resolution body, no GamStop and no fund-segregation requirement.

What can the Gambling Commission do if a casino will not pay me?

If it licenses the operator: a great deal. You escalate through the operator's complaints process to an approved alternative dispute resolution body, free of charge, and the Commission can impose conditions, fines or licence revocation. If it does not license the operator — which is the case for every site on this website — it has no jurisdiction and cannot act for you. Your route is a complaint to whichever authority issued the operator's licence.

Do I have to pay tax on gambling winnings in the UK?

No. Gambling winnings are not taxable income for UK residents, whether the operator is licensed in Britain or offshore. Gambling duty is levied on operators rather than customers, there is no threshold to declare and nothing to put on a self-assessment return. The narrow exceptions — gambling as a trade, and capital gains on cryptocurrency held after a withdrawal — are covered on the gambling tax page.

What is the legal gambling age in the UK?

18 for all forms of commercial gambling, including online casinos, betting and the National Lottery. The offence of permitting underage gambling is committed by the operator, and UK-licensed sites must verify age before allowing a deposit. Offshore sites usually verify later, at the first withdrawal, which is one of several reasons their protections around young people are weaker.

Are the new £5 slot stake limits mandatory everywhere?

They apply to operators holding a UK Gambling Commission licence — £5 per online slot spin for adults and £2 for 18 to 24 year olds. They do not apply to offshore operators, which is why sites outside the UK system carry no stake cap at all. The same is true of the 2.5-second minimum spin speed and the ban on autoplay: both are UK licence conditions, and both disappear when you leave the UK market.

Is GamStop a legal requirement?

It is a condition of holding a UK Gambling Commission remote licence, so every UK-licensed online operator must participate. It is not a criminal law provision, and it has no reach over operators the Commission does not license — which is exactly why "non-GamStop casinos" exist as a category. Registration is free, takes minutes, and covers every UK-licensed site at once for six months, one year or five years.

Does UK gambling law apply in Northern Ireland?

Not directly. The Gambling Act 2005 covers Great Britain — England, Scotland and Wales. Northern Ireland is governed by the Betting, Gaming, Lotteries and Amusements (Northern Ireland) Order 1985, amended in 2022, which does not license online gambling in the same way. In practice Northern Irish players use the same UK-licensed sites and GamStop covers them, but the underlying statutory framework differs.
